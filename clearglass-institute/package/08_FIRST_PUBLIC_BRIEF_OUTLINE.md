## Purpose

Give a reader with no technical background a working understanding of what AI systems are doing when they meet one, the ways those systems create risk for a person or a community, and a short list of things the reader can do this week. The brief informs. It does not sell, alarm, or predict.

## Intended audience

- Adults and older students with no background in computing
- Staff and volunteers of community organizations and small organizations
- Librarians, teachers and others who are asked these questions by the people they serve

Assume the reader uses a phone, email and at least one AI chat tool, and has heard the terms but not had them explained.

## Learning outcomes

After reading, a person should be able to:

1. Describe in one or two sentences what an AI system of the kind they use is doing when it produces an answer.
2. Name the main ways such a system can be wrong, and explain why a confident answer is not evidence of a correct one.
3. Recognize three situations in which AI raises a safety risk for them: impersonation and fraud, exposure of personal information, and reliance on unverified output for an important decision.
4. Apply a short checklist before trusting, sharing or acting on AI output.
5. Know where to report a fraud or a privacy concern in Canada.

## Source-verification checklist

For every source:

- [ ] The author opened the source itself, not a summary of it
- [ ] It is a primary source where one exists: legislation, a regulator, a standards body, a peer-reviewed paper, or the original incident report
- [ ] Publisher, title, publication date and link are recorded
- [ ] The date the author opened it is recorded
- [ ] The sentence in the brief says no more than the source says
- [ ] Any number is copied exactly, with its unit, date and the population it describes
- [ ] The source checker opened the link independently and confirmed the sentence against it
- [ ] The source is not a vendor's marketing page
- [ ] A source that could not be opened or confirmed is removed, together with the sentence that relied on it

For the brief as a whole:

- [ ] Every factual sentence has a numbered source
- [ ] No statistic, quotation, case study or testimonial appears without a source
- [ ] Nothing depends on a single source where a second independent one is available

## Editorial standards

1. **Plain language.** Short sentences. Define every term a careful non-specialist would not know, the first time it appears.
2. **Accuracy over effect.** No predictions, no superlatives, no claims about how common or how costly a risk is unless a source gives the figure.
3. **Defensive only.** Explain how to recognize and avoid a threat. Do not give steps for carrying one out.
4. **No promotion.** No product, service or company is recommended. Nothing links to ClearGlass Inc.
5. **Human responsibility.** The named author reads every sentence and is responsible for it, including any sentence drafted with the help of software. [DECISION REQUIRED] whether the Institute discloses drafting assistance in each brief.
6. **Third-party material.** No text, image or chart is reproduced from another publisher without a licence or written permission. Short quotations are marked and sourced. [LEGAL REVIEW] the rule for images and longer excerpts.
7. **People.** No individual is named or described in a way that identifies them unless they are a public figure acting in a public role and the fact is sourced.
8. **Corrections.** An error is fixed in place and recorded in the corrections log with the date, what was wrong and what it now says.
9. **Review.** Each brief is reviewed at least once a year, and sooner if the facts change.
